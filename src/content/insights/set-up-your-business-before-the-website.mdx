Most people start a business the wrong way round. They buy a domain, pick a logo, ask someone to build a website, and only then think about the company behind it. A few months later they find the business name on the site doesn't match the bank account, the address on the invoices is their home, and the payment provider wants paperwork they don't have.

None of this is hard to fix. It's just easier to do in the right order. This is the order we suggest to new clients before we build anything.

## Decide what the business legally is

Before anything public goes live, decide whether you're trading on your own or as a company. Many small owners choose a limited company or an LLC, because it keeps business debts and risks separate from personal ones and makes the business easier to open accounts for.

The right structure depends on where you are, what you sell, and how you want to be taxed. A short call with an accountant is worth it here. The website can wait a week. A wrong structure is much more annoying to undo later.

## Register the company properly

If you form an LLC or a company, you'll usually need to file formation documents and name a **registered agent**: a person or service with a fixed address who receives official and legal mail for the business during working hours.

You can often act as your own agent, but then your home address goes on public records and you have to be available to receive papers. That's why many owners use a registered agent service instead.

## Get the name, domain and handles together

Check the name in three places on the same day:

- The company register, so the legal name is available
- A domain registrar, ideally for the short .com or your local ending
- The social platforms you plan to use

It's worth adjusting the name slightly if that's what it takes to get a clean match. Customers find you more easily when the company, the website and the social pages all say the same thing.

## Set up the money side

Open a business bank account in the company's name, and keep personal spending out of it from day one. Then pick how you'll take payments online: Stripe, PayPal, Shopify Payments or whatever suits what you sell.

Payment providers usually ask for your company details and a website that shows what you sell, your terms and a contact method. This is one of the reasons it helps to have the company sorted before the site launches.

## Use a business email on your own domain

An address like hello@yourbusiness.com looks more trustworthy than a free webmail address, and it stays with the business if staff change. Google Workspace and Microsoft 365 both make this simple. Set up SPF, DKIM and DMARC records at the same time so your emails don't land in spam.

## Now build the website

With the company, name, email and payments ready, the website brief becomes much clearer. You know the legal name for the footer, the address for the contact page, the payment provider for checkout, and the email for enquiries.

Keep the first version focused: who you help, what you offer, proof that you're real, and one clear way to get in touch. You can add more once you know what customers actually ask about. ## A quick checklist

- Business structure decided, with advice if you need it
- Company registered and a registered agent in place
- Name matched across the register, domain and social handles
- Business bank account and payment provider ready
- Business email on your own domain, with SPF, DKIM and DMARC
- Website brief written with the real details above
